\subsubsection{Relative criteria: taking a choice from a curve}
\label{sec:measure:relative}

\paragraph{Overview}
\label{sec:measure:relative:overview}
A relative criterion does not score one partition: it reads a sequence of
scores and selects from it. The sequence comes from evaluating an internal
index over candidate partitions --- typically the same method at each
cluster count~$k$ --- and the criterion returns the~$k$ the curve points
to. Two are implemented. \textsc{MaxCriterion} takes the best-scoring
candidate; \textsc{ElbowCriterion} takes the candidate of greatest
curvature, for the monotone curves on which a maximum is uninformative.
The rule is kept separate from the index it reads, because the same curve
yields different answers under different rules, and which rule was used is
part of the result.

\paragraph{Definition}
\label{sec:measure:relative:definition}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ be the swept candidates and $s\colon \mathcal{K}\to
\mathbb{R}$ their scores under a fixed internal index, whose direction is
given by a relation $\succ$ (read ``is better than'').
\textsc{MaxCriterion} returns $\arg\max^{\succ}_{k\in\mathcal{K}} s(k)$ ---
the optimum \emph{in the direction the index declares}, not the numerical
maximum. \textsc{ElbowCriterion} is geometric: order the candidates, form
the points $(k, s(k))$, rescale both axes to the unit square, and let
$\ell$ be the chord from the first point to the last, the curve a constant
marginal return would trace. The elbow is
\begin{align}
    \arg\max_{k\in\mathcal{K}} \operatorname{dist}\big((k, s(k)),\, \ell\big),
\end{align}
the candidate lying furthest from that chord. The construction is the
distance-to-chord method of~\cite{ref_27}; reading~$k$ off a criterion
curve goes back to~\cite{ref_26}.

\paragraph{Properties}
\label{sec:measure:relative:properties}
\textsc{MaxCriterion} carries no direction of its own: it selects through
the index's \texttt{is\_better}, so a maximised index yields the peak and a
minimised one the trough, with no code change. The index is required at
construction with no default, for the same reason its direction must be
declared --- a guessed direction eventually selects the worst candidate
and reports it as the best. Ties break by sweep order, returning the
smallest~$k$ achieving the best score. \textsc{ElbowCriterion} is
direction-free, since distance from a chord does not depend on the index's
direction, which is why an elbow can be read from a minimised curve
without inverting it. Both axes are normalised first, so selection is
invariant to the index's units and to the sweep width. A failed run enters
the curve as \texttt{NaN} and is kept, so the run is still reported, and
\texttt{is\_better} guarantees it never wins.

\paragraph{Applicability}
\label{sec:measure:relative:applicability}
Both apply wherever partitions are compared on a common index.
\textsc{MaxCriterion} accepts any candidate type, comparing only scores;
\textsc{ElbowCriterion} needs numeric candidates, because it measures a
distance along the swept axis, and raises on a non-numeric sweep rather
than returning a meaningless answer. Noise handling is inherited: where
the index beneath excludes noise-labelled observations, so does the curve,
and that exclusion is stated wherever the selection is reported.

\paragraph{Computation and complexity}
\label{sec:measure:relative:complexity}
Given the curve, both are linear in $|\mathcal{K}|$. The cost lies in
producing it, at one clustering fit and one index evaluation per
candidate; where the index needs the full pairwise distance matrix, as
silhouette does, that evaluation is quadratic in the number of
observations and is the binding constraint at \projectname{} data volumes.

\paragraph{Application to \projectname{} data}
\label{sec:measure:relative:application}
The criteria are registered as \texttt{"max"} and \texttt{"elbow"}. They
decide only: the sweep generating the candidates lives in the selection
subpackage, and the curve is drawn by the selection-curve figure, which
marks the choice whether or not it was conclusive. \textsc{MaxCriterion}
declares \texttt{flat\_tolerance} and \texttt{min\_margin}, each a fraction
of the index's declared range; \textsc{ElbowCriterion} declares
\texttt{min\_curvature}, a fraction of the chord length. They are
parameters rather than constants so the write-up can state the threshold a
selection had to clear. The accompanying notebook evidences the behaviour
end to end.

\paragraph{Behaviour}
\label{sec:measure:relative:behaviour}
Demonstrated on the iris benchmark, whose structure is known. Sweeping
$k=2,\dots,10$, the silhouette curve peaks at $k=2$ and
\textsc{MaxCriterion} selects it; replacing the index with a minimised
copy makes the identical criterion select the trough at $k=9$, confirming
the direction is the index's. The sum-of-squares curve falls
monotonically, so its numerical best is always the largest~$k$ swept:
\textsc{MaxCriterion} returns the sweep boundary $k=10$, while
\textsc{ElbowCriterion} returns the bend at $k=4$. That is the behaviour
the pair exists to provide.

\paragraph{Limitations}
\label{sec:measure:relative:limitations}
A criterion can always return an answer, but it is not always warranted,
and \texttt{is\_conclusive} reports when it is not. For
\textsc{MaxCriterion} the curve is inconclusive with fewer than three
candidates; when the spread falls below \texttt{flat\_tolerance}, the
candidates being indistinguishable; when the margin over the runner-up
falls below \texttt{min\_margin}, a tie rather than a peak; or when the
curve is monotone with its best value at a sweep boundary, an artefact of
where the sweep stopped and the case the elbow answers. For
\textsc{ElbowCriterion} it is inconclusive when the greatest departure
from the chord falls below \texttt{min\_curvature} --- a straight line has
no elbow, and reporting one turns rounding into a finding. One limitation
is inherited: a criterion is only as neutral as the index beneath it, so a
shape-biased index carries that bias into the selection. That is a threat
to validity for Sect.~\ref{sec:methodology:threats}, not a detail.

\paragraph{Summary}
\label{sec:measure:relative:summary}
\textsc{MaxCriterion} is the rule for a peaked curve and
\textsc{ElbowCriterion} the rule for a monotone one. Both defer direction
and reporting to the index they read, and both declare when the curve does
not support a choice. They are the mechanism by which~$k$ is chosen
wherever a sweep is reported, per Sect.~\ref{sec:methodology:metrics}.
